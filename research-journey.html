Questions, turning points & research leadership

My research journey

From eye tracking and medical imaging to explainable sequential decision-making from uncertain evidence.

The question running through the work

How do people and models interpret imperfect evidence,
and what should happen next?

My research began with how people look at and interpret information. Working in medical AI brought questions about learning from limited data and clinical expertise. Personalised digital health introduced decisions over time. Today, these questions meet in my work on explainable sequential decision-making from uncertain evidence.

  1. 01

    2010–2014
    Dundee

    Computing, cognitive science & eye tracking

    How do we interpret
    what we see?

    At the University of Dundee, I studied Computing and Cognitive Science and worked in the Communication and Eye-Tracking Lab. We investigated language comprehension, including how a mismatch between language and an image affects where people look.

    My bachelor’s project became my first medical-imaging study: comparing five measures of retinal vessel tortuosity with expert assessments. This led to my first publication in 2014 and an enduring interest in the relationship between computational measurements and human interpretation.

    The question that carried forwardWhen do a measurement and an expert judgement describe the same thing?

  2. 02

    2014–2020
    Edinburgh

    Toshiba / Canon Medical Research Europe & Heriot-Watt

    Clinical expertise as part
    of the learning process

    A medical-imaging internship in Edinburgh led to an industrial Engineering Doctorate with Heriot-Watt University and Toshiba, later Canon Medical Research Europe. I worked on learning from limited medical data, including wearable signals, stroke-sign detection in CT, and representations for retrieving similar patients.

    For stroke detection, clinical knowledge helped shape how we compared corresponding brain regions and represented anatomical context. After the doctorate, I stayed in industry and led projects in active and continual learning, alongside contributions to clinical-text analysis.

    Clinician annotation time was a practical constraint. We studied which cases a clinician should label next, and how a model could acquire new capabilities while retaining what it had already learned. I supervised master’s researchers in this work, which contributed to publications, patents, and patent applications.

    The question that carried forwardWhich evidence should we seek next, and how can clinical expertise guide learning?
    Active learning for lung nodule segmentation · 2019 ↗Continual learning for thoracic segmentation · 2020 ↗
  3. 03

    2020–2024
    Poznań & Sano

    A shift towards everyday health decisions

    What support should this
    person receive next?

    The pandemic prompted me to think more about personal health, wearables, and the support people need outside clinical appointments. At Poznan University of Technology, I joined CAPABLE, developing personalised coaching and decision support for cancer patients living at home during treatment.

    This brought reinforcement learning into my work. The question became which activity or message to offer, and when, given a person’s changing context and previous responses. Wearable signals could inform the context, while responses to prompts and completion of activities provided feedback.

    At Sano, I developed a digital-health-intervention research agenda and led projects on engagement with mobile and wearable applications. I also completed an MBA with mobile health in mind. These experiences sharpened a distinction that continues to shape my research: app engagement, intervention adherence, and health outcomes are different quantities. Their relationship needs testing.

    The question that carried forwardAre we optimising what is easy to record, or the outcome we actually value?
    SATO: designing patient-centred interventions · 2023 ↗Reinforcement learning for timely prompts · Synthetic-data feasibility study ↗
  4. 04

    Since April 2024
    Amsterdam

    VU Computer Science & Cancer Center Amsterdam UMC

    A bridge between methods
    and clinical questions

    I joined a position explicitly conceived as a bridge between VU’s AI research and Cancer Center Amsterdam. I initially expected to concentrate on patient-facing reinforcement learning. That remains part of my work, but close collaboration with clinicians also brought me back to medical imaging.

    Images provide evidence at diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up. My role is to help formulate joint research questions, develop projects, and supervise work across disciplines. The overarching objective is to maximise survival and quality of life. I frame AI across the patient journey as a sequential-decision problem: estimate the patient state, predict possible transitions given actions, and evaluate the longer-term consequences. Patient support and clinician-facing imaging address complementary parts of this problem.

    One collaboration studied survival and quality-of-life trajectories in pancreatic cancer despite incomplete follow-up. I brought together AI researchers and clinicians, formulated and conducted the work, and coordinated clinical feedback. Observed trajectories describe patient courses; they do not establish what would have happened under an alternative treatment.

    The question that carried forwardWhat could AI change at different points in care, and how would we know that it helped?
    CAREPATH · I lead the reinforcement learning contribution ↗PULSE-ONC · Explainability and treatment monitoring ↗
  5. 05

    Ongoing research
    CLARITY

    Estimating patient state from variable evidence

    Could variability itself
    carry information?

    Working with clinicians made a difficulty in sequential decisions especially clear: the patient state is not directly observed. Measurements vary, sampling matters, and readers may interpret difficult images differently. We form an estimate from the evidence available, and uncertainty affects both that estimate and the changes we infer between visits.

    These discussions led to CLARITY. I led the application and lead the joint work across AI, radiology, radiotherapy, and surgery. We study tumour volume and boundary uncertainty over time, comparing uncertainty estimates with reader variability and testing associations with pathological response and outcomes.

    The scientific hypothesis is that the difficulty of defining a tumour boundary may carry information about response. It is not an established biomarker, and lower uncertainty is not assumed to mean a better response.

    The question that carries forwardHow should uncertain assessments inform the next clinical decision?

  6. 06

    Future direction
    Proposed studies

    Returning to eye tracking, with decisions as the endpoint

    When AI is wrong,
    what helps a clinician notice?

    My proposed explanation studies return to where the journey began: gaze and interpretation. I want to examine how explanation methods relate to the evidence radiologists attend to, the findings they describe, and the decisions they make.

    Looking in the same place does not establish that a person and a model reason in the same way. The work therefore distinguishes spatial alignment, clinical meaning, and faithfulness to model behaviour.

    The aim is to develop and test explanations that help clinicians use AI appropriately: benefiting from correct advice while recognising misleading advice. Diagnostic decisions, reading time, confidence, and burden would be evaluated. Closer agreement with gaze is a property to investigate, not evidence of benefit by itself.

    These are proposed studies, subject to funding and study setup. They are distinct from CLARITY’s ongoing treatment-response research.

    The thread comes full circle.

    Understanding attention is the starting point.
    Understanding its effect on decisions is the next question.
