The scientific vision

What sequence of decisions
leads to better patient outcomes?

The goal is measurable: survival and patient-reported quality of life over time. My programme asks how AI introduced at different points in care could contribute to those outcomes, from diagnosis and treatment selection to monitoring, patient support, and recovery.

Reinforcement learning provides a framework for studying policies that optimise expected long-term outcomes. This requires estimating the patient’s current state, modelling possible state transitions given actions, and learning how the consequences of one decision affect the next.

Uncertainty and explainability are integral to this problem: what do the observations tell us, how reliable are predicted consequences, and how can clinicians and patients use that evidence appropriately?

Three connected research pillars

Understanding, reliance, and action

The pillars serve one programme objective: better survival and quality of life. They connect uncertainty in patient-state estimates, the consequences of actions, and how people use AI in care decisions.

Conceptual illustrations of the research themes.

The sequential-decision framework

Patient state. Possible transitions.
Actions with long-term consequences.

AI can contribute at each stage of care by updating what we know about a patient and evaluating what could happen next under different actions.

  1. 01 · Observe

    Gather evidence

    Images, signals, patient reports, and treatment history provide observations across the care journey.

  2. 02 · Estimate

    Infer patient state

    Represent the current state as an uncertainty-aware estimate, rather than assume it is fully observed.

  3. 03 · Predict

    Model transitions

    Estimate how patient state may change given a candidate action, including treatment or supportive intervention.

  4. 04 · Decide

    Evaluate actions

    Study policies that account for consequences over time and support clinically appropriate choices.

Long-term optimisation objective

Maximise survival and quality of life

Evaluate survival and patient-reported quality of life. Test how intermediate measures, including response, toxicity, adherence, and engagement, relate to these outcomes.

Actions are followed by new observations and feedback, informing the next state estimate and decision.

This is the research vision. Current projects investigate parts of the framework; an end-to-end oncology policy and its effect on patient outcomes remain to be developed and validated.

Looking ahead · 2027–2031

A research agenda for
AI-supported human decisions

I aim to connect state estimation, action-conditioned transition modelling, and explainable sequential decisions with evaluation of survival and quality of life.

  1. 01

    Explanations that support appropriate reliance

    Compare explanations with radiologists’ gaze and diagnostic reasoning, while separately testing faithfulness to model behaviour. Evaluate effects on diagnostic choices, reading time, confidence, and burden, especially when AI advice is wrong.

    Research question: can an explanation help a clinician detect a mistake, rather than make wrong advice more persuasive?

  2. 02

    Adaptive support under partial observability

    Develop uncertainty-aware patient-state estimates and models of transitions given actions. Study how reinforcement learning can use them to optimise expected longer-term outcomes, including the consequences of intervention timing.

    Research question: how should uncertainty in patient state influence the next action?

  3. 03

    Evaluation across models, people, and outcomes

    Connect technical evaluation and human decision studies with prospective evaluation of survival and patient-reported quality of life. Test whether intermediate improvements translate into the programme’s long-term objectives.

    Research question: when does a better model lead to a better decision?

My role & motivation

A bridge between AI research
and cancer care

At VU Computer Science and Cancer Center Amsterdam UMC, my role is to develop joint research questions, build projects, and supervise work across disciplines. Patient-facing reinforcement learning and clinician-facing imaging provide complementary settings for the same scientific questions.

My route here began with eye-tracking research on language comprehension, continued through industrial medical AI and personalised health support, and returned to imaging through clinical collaborations. It taught me to ask not only whether a model predicts well, but whether we are measuring and optimising the outcome that matters.
